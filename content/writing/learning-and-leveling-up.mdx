I think programming is one of the most broadly useful things a person can learn,
and I don't mean that in the "learn to code, get a job" sense. Strip away the
syntax and the tooling and what's left is problem decomposition and logical
reasoning: take something big and vague, break it into pieces small enough to
reason about, and put them back together so they actually work. That skill pays
off in law, medicine, plumbing, running a kitchen — anywhere. It's why I think
kids should learn it early, in a friendly language like Python or JavaScript, the
same way we teach arithmetic. Not because they'll all become engineers, but
because "break the problem down, then reason through it" is a life skill that
happens to be teachable through code.

So this is really two essays. One for the person starting out, and one for the
engineer who's competent and wondering why the growth slowed down.

## Learning it in the first place

The single most common way people fail to learn programming is by trying to learn
*about* it instead of *doing* it. Courses, videos, and books feel like progress —
you're absorbing information — but the skill isn't information. It's the ability to
sit in front of a blank file and a broken program and reason your way forward.
That's a muscle, and you only build it under load.

A few things I'd tell anyone starting:

- **Build things you actually want to exist.** Motivation beats optimal curriculum
  every time. A janky tool you care about will teach you more than a polished
  course you're grinding through out of duty.
- **Get comfortable being stuck.** Being stuck isn't a sign you're failing at
  programming — being stuck *is* programming. The job is largely the practiced art
  of being confused and working your way out of it methodically.
- **Read errors like clues, not verdicts.** A beginner sees a stack trace as "I
  broke it." An experienced person sees a fairly specific message telling them
  where to look next. The trace is on your side.

The whole game at this stage is closing the loop between an idea in your head and
a thing running on the screen, over and over, until decomposition starts to feel
automatic.

## Leveling up after the basics

Here's the thing nobody warns you about: the skills that get you from beginner to
competent are almost the *opposite* of the ones that get you from competent to
senior. Early on it's all about the code — syntax, algorithms, making the thing
work. Later, the code is the easy part. The hard part becomes judgment.

**Optimize for depth of understanding, not breadth of familiarity.** It's tempting
to collect frameworks like merit badges. But someone who deeply understands how
one database, one language, and one runtime actually work will run circles around
someone with a shallow acquaintance with twelve. Depth transfers; breadth mostly
doesn't. When you understand *why* something works, the next tool is a translation
exercise instead of a fresh start.

**Learn to read code, not just write it.** You'll spend far more of your career
reading than writing. The engineers who level up fastest are voracious readers of
other people's code — the framework you depend on, the standard library, the
messy service nobody wants to touch. Every codebase you read is a set of decisions
someone made under constraints, and reverse-engineering the *why* is how you build
taste.

**Chase the problems just past your reach.** You grow at the edge of your
competence, not in the comfortable middle. The task you're pretty sure you can do
teaches you almost nothing. Volunteer for the one that scares you slightly.

<Callout type="tip" title="A concrete drill">
  Take a piece of software you use and admire, and try to rebuild a small slice of
  it from scratch — a toy version of a build tool, a router, a state manager.
  You'll understand the real thing at a depth no tutorial delivers, because you'll
  have hit the same walls its authors did.
</Callout>

## Where the coding agents fit

I can't write this in 2026 and skip the obvious question: if an AI can write the
code, why build any of this the hard way?

I use coding agents constantly, and they're genuinely one of the biggest
productivity multipliers I've seen in my career. But *multiplier* is the operative
word. They multiply the judgment you already have — and they multiply zero to
zero. An agent will happily generate a plausible, confident, subtly wrong solution
to a problem you don't understand, and if you can't tell, you'll ship it. The
person who has done the reasoning themselves knows what to ask for, recognizes
when the answer is off, and catches the failure the model couldn't. The person who
skipped that has outsourced not just the typing but the thinking, and it shows the
moment anything goes non-standard.

So the leveling-up advice doesn't change in the age of agents. If anything it
sharpens: the mechanical parts are increasingly automated, which makes the
irreplaceable parts — deep domain knowledge, hard-won judgment, the ability to
reason about a problem nobody has solved before — *more* valuable, not less. The
tool is extraordinary. It is not a substitute for knowing what you're doing, and I
don't expect it to become one.

Learn to reason. Learn it deeply. Then use every tool you can get your hands on to
go faster — including the ones that write code for you. Just make sure you're the
one who could have written it.
