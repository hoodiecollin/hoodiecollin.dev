Early in my career I assumed the job was the job. A senior engineer at a
ten-person startup and a senior engineer at a Fortune 500 were doing the same
work at different addresses. After a decade of doing both, I don't believe that
anymore. The title is a constant; almost nothing under it is.

If you're choosing where to work — or trying to figure out why the place you're
at feels the way it does — it helps to know what each stage actually asks of
you.

## The startup

At a startup, the scarce resource is certainty. Nobody knows if the product
should exist, whether the customer will pay, or if the company will be alive in
a year. Your job is to reduce that uncertainty as fast as possible, and code is
just one of the tools you use to do it.

That means the work is broad and shallow by necessity. You'll write the
frontend, stand up the backend, argue about pricing, talk to a customer, and fix
the deploy pipeline — often in the same day. The best startup engineers are
comfortable being *responsible for outcomes they can't fully control*. You ship
something, watch what happens, and change your mind quickly.

The trap is mistaking motion for progress. Startups reward speed, and speed with
no feedback loop is just burning runway in a straight line. The engineers who
thrive aren't the fastest typists; they're the ones with the judgment to know
which corners are safe to cut and which ones will sink the company in six months.

<Callout type="note" title="Who this suits">
  People who want ownership more than stability, who learn by doing, and who can
  hold ambiguity without freezing. If you need a clear spec to feel productive, a
  startup will feel like chaos — because it is.
</Callout>

## The scale-up

The mid-size company is the most interesting stage, and the most volatile. The
product works. Customers are paying. Now the problem changes from *"does this
exist?"* to *"can this survive its own success?"*

This is where the systems you build actually start to matter, because other
people are building on top of them. The proof-of-concept that made the company
money is now load-bearing, and every shortcut from the startup days is coming due
with interest. Much of the real work at this stage is *re-architecting things
that already work* without stopping the business that depends on them.

It's also where the org chart starts to have opinions. You go from "everyone
does everything" to teams, on-call rotations, and the first real process. Some of
that process is the immune system protecting a company that can no longer afford
to break; some of it is bureaucracy metastasizing early. Learning to tell those
two apart is one of the most valuable skills you can develop here.

## The enterprise

At a large company, the scarce resource is coordination. The technology is rarely
the hard part — the hard part is that any meaningful change touches six teams,
three of which you've never met, one of which is in a timezone twelve hours off.

Enterprise engineering gets a bad reputation, and some of it is earned: the
feedback loops are long, the blast radius of a mistake is enormous, so everything
moves deliberately. But the reputation misses what's genuinely hard and worth
learning. Making a system reliable at a scale where "one in a million" happens a
thousand times a day is a real craft. So is landing a change cleanly across an
organization that will still be running your code long after you've left.

The failure mode here is going to sleep. It's entirely possible to collect a good
paycheck for years without your judgment ever being tested. The engineers who
keep growing at large companies are the ones who deliberately seek out the
ambiguous, cross-cutting problems that the org structure would rather nobody
own.

## What to optimize for

None of these is better. They're different games, and the mistake is playing one
by the rules of another — bringing startup cowboy instincts to a system a million
people depend on, or bringing enterprise process to a company that needs to find
product-market fit before the money runs out.

What I'd tell my younger self:

- **Optimize for the size of your feedback loop, not the size of the logo.** You
  grow fastest where you can see the consequences of your decisions and are
  forced to own them.
- **Match the stage to what you need to learn.** Want range and ownership? Go
  small. Want to learn how systems fail at scale? Go big. Want to watch an
  organization form in real time? The scale-up is a front-row seat.
- **Whatever the stage, find the ambiguity.** The work that grows you is the work
  nobody has fully scoped yet. Every stage has it; you just have to look in a
  different place.

The title on your badge will say the same thing in all three places. Don't let it
fool you into thinking you already know the job.
