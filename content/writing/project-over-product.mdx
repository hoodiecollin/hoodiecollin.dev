---
title: "Project management matters more than product management"
description: "Product management gets the vision and the credit. Project management is what actually ships software. A defense of the unglamorous discipline that holds everything together."
date: "2026-07-19"
---

This is going to sound like heresy in a certain kind of company, so let me say it
plainly: on most teams I've worked on, good project management was more decisive
to whether we shipped than good product management. Not less important as a
person — more decisive as a function. The two roles get blurred together, often
into one overworked human, and when they're separated it's the product side that
gets the glory. I think we have it backwards.

Let me be careful about what I'm claiming, because it's easy to misread.

## The distinction people skip

**Product management is about deciding what to build and why.** Vision, strategy,
prioritization, understanding the customer, saying no to good ideas so the great
ones have room. This is real, hard, valuable work.

**Project management is about making the building actually happen.** Sequencing
the work, surfacing dependencies before they become blockers, keeping the six
people who need to talk to each other actually talking, noticing that the thing
due Friday quietly slipped on Tuesday. It's the connective tissue between a plan
and a shipped result.

Here's the asymmetry I keep running into: a team with a mediocre product direction
and excellent project management will still *ship something*, learn from it, and
correct. A team with brilliant product vision and no project management ships
nothing, learns nothing, and slowly convinces itself that the problem is a lack of
vision — so it generates more vision, which also doesn't ship.

<Callout type="note" title="Why the credit is lopsided">
  Product management is legible. It produces roadmaps, decks, and a clean story
  you can tell in an all-hands. Good project management is nearly invisible — its
  output is the *absence* of chaos, and nobody applauds a fire that didn't happen.
  We reward what's easy to see, not what's load-bearing.
</Callout>

## The unglamorous work is the work

I've come to believe that a huge fraction of what determines whether software
ships has nothing to do with the code or the vision. It's whether someone noticed
that two teams were blocked on each other and nobody had said so out loud. Whether
the dependency on the third-party vendor got flagged in week one instead of week
nine. Whether the definition of "done" was agreed on before, not litigated after.

That work is deeply unglamorous, and it is done by the people who tend to be
invisible in the org's status hierarchy — project managers, yes, but also the QA
engineers who find the problem before the customer does, and the customer-facing
folks who translate a vague complaint into an actionable bug. These people are the
unsung heroes of every launch I've ever been proud of, and it is not close. A
department stacked with brilliant programmers and nobody minding the connective
tissue is a department that ships late, ships broken, or doesn't ship.

## What this means if you build teams

I'm not arguing against product management. A team with no product sense builds
beautifully-executed things nobody wants — that's a real and painful failure mode.
The point is one of *balance and respect*, because the industry's defaults are
skewed.

- **Fund the connective tissue on purpose.** Project management is not overhead to
  be absorbed by whoever has spare cycles. When it's nobody's explicit job, it
  becomes everybody's implicit and resented job, done badly.
- **Give credit where the work actually is.** If your recognition all flows to
  vision and none to execution, you're training your best executors to stop
  executing and start producing decks.
- **Respect the whole department.** The health of an engineering org is not the
  average talent of its programmers. It's how well programmers, QA, project
  management, and the customer-facing side actually function *as one system*. The
  weak link is almost never where the prestige is pointing.

The most reliable teams I've been on weren't the ones with the most visionary
product leaders. They were the ones where somebody, with or without the title,
quietly made sure the trains ran. That job deserves a lot more respect than it
gets.
